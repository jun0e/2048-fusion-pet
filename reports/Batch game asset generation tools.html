2048 Siege research report · rendered from Batch game asset generation tools.md

Let GPT Image 2.5 paint 2048 Siege

Use OpenAI's GPT Image 2.5 as the pipeline's primary image model, use Microsoft's MAI-Image-2.6 (reached through one OpenRouter key) as the backup, and treat the Vertex/Nano Banana 2 lane already built into the pipeline as a free bonus rather than the plan. GPT Image 2.5 is the only model that does both of the hardest jobs: it tops the multi-image editing leaderboards, the closest public stand-in for two-parent fusions, and it returns truly transparent PNGs, while OpenAI's Batch API halves the price of its reference-driven edits. A full pass of about 140 images (117 assets plus 20% retries) at "high" quality should cost roughly $5–8 through Batch or $10–15 at standard rates; the range is wide because OpenAI does not publish how many tokens a reference image consumes. MAI-Image-2.6 ranks third on Artificial Analysis's editing board, level with Nano Banana 2.1 and ahead of the Nano Banana 2 that the Vertex lane uses, at about $5.45 per pass. Google's failures are billing walls, not model weaknesses: no Gemini image model has a free tier, AI Pro users in Vietnam, India and South Korea cannot switch off the Gemini app's sparkle, and the Vertex lane depends on a $10 credit whose attachment rests on a single support-forum reply and, at Nano Banana 2 prices, leaves the budget guard room for about two retries a month. No benchmark tests sticker-style creatures, so every leaderboard lead below is a starting assumption rather than proof: spend about $6 on a ten-image test of the hardest jobs before any full pass, then give each asset class to one model.

Google's blocks are billing walls, and the one open lane is narrow

Every Google failure seen this session traces to billing state rather than to what the models can do. Google's own price list states that "No image model has a free tier" (Gemini API pricing), which is exactly why a free-tier AI Studio key returns an image quota of zero even for Nano Banana 2 Lite. Paid AI Studio access starts with a $5 minimum prepaid purchase (Gemini API billing), and whether the AI Pro credit can then pay for those calls is disputed: Google Support told a subscriber in June 2026 that Developer Program credits "cannot be transferred to the PrePay Payments Account" (Google AI Developers Forum), and the AI Studio playground's AI Pro allowance answered "permission denied" here. The sparkle on the user's Gemini app images follows the same pattern. The watermark toggle Google shipped in August 2026 appears for users in India, South Korea and Vietnam only on AI Ultra (Gemini Apps Help; 9to5Google), and Flow stamps a visible mark automatically on residents of those countries (Google Flow Help). Neither app has an API, so neither could feed the Node pipeline even without the mark.

Vertex express mode is the one Google lane that clears both walls on paper. In the same June thread, Support steered the subscriber to Vertex because its charges land on a postpaid billing account and are "fully eligible to be automatically deducted from… your monthly $10.00 Google Cloud credit balance", but that reply is the only evidence the credit attaches, and no Google documentation confirms it. Express mode is also a Preview product: it lists gemini-3.1-flash-image and gemini-3-pro-image with "Dynamic" rate limits, omits Nano Banana 2.1, and withdraws its 90-day free tier from existing Cloud users and anyone who enables billing (Agent Platform express mode), so in practice the user gets the free tier or the credit, not both. Even if everything works, the arithmetic is tight. At $0.067 per Nano Banana 2 image, the $7.84 pass nearly fills the guard's $8 stop line (80% of the $10 cap) and leaves room for about two retries per 30 days, while developers building sprite sets report needing two or three tries on problem frames (OpenAI community). The pipeline's usage ledger is still empty, so neither backend has yet returned a paid image.

None of this makes Nano Banana a weak model, and the case for staying deserves a fair hearing. Nano Banana 2 ranks first on TRACE-Bench, the newest multi-reference benchmark, although that benchmark never tested GPT Image 2.x (arXiv 2608.16765); Nano Banana 2.1 accepts up to 14 references split into character, object and style slots (Gemini API image docs); and the casual mobile studio Nukebox reports near-final renders in one or two attempts with Gemini, against five or more with earlier tools (Scenario case study). The cheapest way to stay with Google is not Vertex at all: a $5 AI Studio prepay unlocks Nano Banana 2.1 at $0.0336 per image, or $0.0168 through Batch (Gemini API pricing), about $4.70 per pass, and the pipeline's Gemini backend already defaults to it. That option is worth knowing, but it means reinvesting in the billing system that has blocked every attempt so far, which is why the plan keeps Google as a free extra rather than its backbone.

GPT Image 2.5 leads multi-image editing and alone returns real alpha

OpenAI shipped GPT Image 2.5 on 8 September 2026 as two variants at identical token prices: Sunburst, built for "precise edits and fine-detail creative work", and Flare, built for speed (Grida; OpenAI pricing). On Arena's multi-image edit leaderboard of 6 October, the board closest to fusing two parent creatures, Sunburst scores 1527 against 1476 for Flare and a preliminary 1431 for Nano Banana 2.1 (Arena; Atlas Cloud); these Arena figures come from search summaries because the page blocked direct fetching. A 96-point gap means Sunburst would win roughly 63% of blind head-to-head votes, a real edge rather than a rout. Artificial Analysis's editing board tells the same story, with Sunburst at 1186, MAI-Image-2.6 at 1143, Nano Banana 2.1 at 1141, Meta's Muse Image at 1123 and Nano Banana 2, the Vertex lane's model, at 1114 (Artificial Analysis).

Three caveats keep that lead from being a verdict. First, Artificial Analysis measured GPT Image 2.5 at its "max" setting, priced at $210.7 per 1,000 images, four times the "high" setting this report budgets for, and no leaderboard ranks 2.5 at "high". Second, OpenAI itself admits an "occasional struggle to maintain visual consistency for recurring characters" (OpenAI guide), and in a reseller's four-prompt test Nano Banana 2.1 won or tied three while Sunburst invented signage nobody asked for (Atlas Cloud). Third, and most important, every identity benchmark measures human faces or photos, never cartoon creatures. Over four successive edits, GPT-Image-2 lost about a quarter of its ArcFace face-identity similarity (0.71 to 0.53) and Nano Banana 2 about 23% (arXiv 2609.04151), and TRACE-Bench finds attribute binding, the skill of tracking which feature came from which reference image, to be the weakest even for its leader, which scores 0.74 (arXiv 2608.16765). Binding is precisely what a fusion of "parent A's body plus one part of parent B" demands, so the test run must check it head-on.

Transparency is where GPT Image 2.5 stands alone among the multi-reference editors. OpenAI's guide tells developers to "set background: 'transparent' and use output_format: 'png' or 'webp'" with either 2.5 model (OpenAI guide), and an OpenAI staff member confirmed on 1 October 2026 that gpt-image-2's transparency preview had been discontinued because the 2.5 family "has more robust transparency support" (OpenAI community). Flat illustration "with hard color boundaries" is native alpha's best case, while glass and glows come back 83–99% opaque instead of see-through (Runware); Nano Banana, MAI, Seedream, FLUX 3 and Muse document no alpha output at all. The advantage is smaller than it looks, because the art style already gives the chroma keyer what it needs. A developer who keyed dark-outlined Nano Banana sprites found brightness-based removal "catastrophic" but pure green, a 2–3 px white border and colour-based (HSV) keying reliable (roboticape), and the pipeline's cream sticker rim is that border. Native alpha therefore matters most for the 17 enemies, whose prompt forbids the rim; the halos are specified as flat, palette-snapped rings that key cleanly (any future soft glow is better rendered on black for additive blending than trusted to either method), and the 28 portraits and the background fill the whole frame in the current job specs and need no alpha at all. One gap remains open: no official page confirms transparency on the edits endpoint with reference images inside a Batch job.

Automation and cost complete the case. OpenAI's Batch API covers /v1/images/edits for both 2.5 variants at 50% off, with a 24-hour window and up to 50,000 requests per batch (OpenAI Batch guide), while synchronous calls are capped at 20 images per minute on the entry tier (OpenAI Flare model page), about seven minutes per pass. Output costs $30 per million tokens ($15 through Batch), which puts a 1024² image at $0.0527 on "high" and $0.0132 on "medium" (Grida); references bill as input at $8 per million tokens (OpenAI pricing), adding an estimated $2–8 per pass for the 200–280 reference images a pass sends, depending on the unpublished token count per image. Adding OpenAI means a third backend beside vertex and gemini in tools/pipeline.mjs, not a rewrite, though its Image API expects multipart file uploads rather than inline base64 and OpenAI requires organization verification before the first GPT Image call (OpenAI guide). The same guide documents no visible watermark and never mentions C2PA provenance metadata, and its moderation: "low" setting leaves cute creatures and chibi foes little to trip over; this research did not re-verify OpenAI's output-ownership clause, so save a copy of the current terms before release.

The Gemini app and Flow (visible sparkle, no API), Midjourney, Adobe Firefly, Reve, Recraft V4 Styles, Ideogram and Stability drop out entirely, for reasons covered below.

MAI-Image-2.6 takes the backup slot as the best-ranked editor outside OpenAI and Google. It trails Sunburst by 43 points on Artificial Analysis's editing board, which means Sunburst would win only about 56% of head-to-head votes, yet it costs $38.9 per 1,000 images against $210.7 for Sunburst at "max", and its Flash sibling ranks fifth at $19.5 (Artificial Analysis). When Artificial Analysis split its editing arena into 17 category boards on 2 September, MAI-Image-2.6's preview led the overall board and seven categories, including Scene and Style Edit (AlphaSignal). Its OpenRouter listing reportedly allows up to five reference images and native 9:16 output (OpenRouter), enough for a fusion plus an extra identity anchor, and for the background. Routing it through OpenRouter's Image API, which takes up to ten input_references and returns a base64 PNG with a usage.cost field (OpenRouter), buys one adapter that also reaches FLUX 3, Muse Image and Grok Imagine, and lets the budget ledger record real spend instead of estimates. Its weaknesses are thin evidence (no practitioner sprite reports, a reference cap known only from a listing snippet, unverified terms) and no alpha.

The remaining API models each have a narrower case. Seedream 5.0 Pro is the identity specialist worth watching: it topped Artificial Analysis's Identity-Preserving Edit category in September (AlphaSignal) and takes 2–10 references as base64 inside JSON, the payload shape the pipeline already sends, but it renders one image per call with no batch mode, and its BytePlus watermark flag controls a visible "AI generated" badge with an ambiguous default, so false must be sent explicitly (mer.vin); it costs $0.045 per image plus $0.003 per extra reference (The Rundown). Muse Image costs $0.01 per image (RuntimeWire), ranks seventh on editing and advertises anchoring "a whole series of generations on a small set of reference images" (Meta), which makes a $1.40 pass the cheapest serious option, though Meta has not published its reference cap. FLUX 3 charges a flat $0.048 with up to ten references free and is marked for commercial use on fal (fal), as is Muse (Let's Data Science), but it misses the editing top 25, defaults to JPEG, and Black Forest Labs offers no batch API and expires results after ten minutes (BFL). Qwen-Image-3.0 ($0.03) and Grok Imagine 2.0 ($0.04 plus $0.01 per input image) both cap input at three images (LLM Reference; muapi), exactly a fusion's load with no slot left for an extra anchor.

Platforms, Figma and local models solve problems this project lacks

The game-asset platforms mostly resell the same frontier models, so what they sell is custom-model training, server-side workflows and bundled background removal rather than better pixels. Scenario trains style LoRAs on 10–30 images and character LoRAs on 5–15, but only from its $45-a-month Pro plan, with edit-model training reserved for the $75 Max plan (Scenario pricing). Layer starts at $10 a month for 300 credits, charges 0.5 credits per background removal (Layer pricing) and assigns customers "all rights, title and interest" in their outputs (Layer FAQ), for an estimated $10–25 per pass, while Leonardo's free tier vests image rights in Leonardo and publishes the outputs (terms.law). LoRAs can only be trained on open bases such as FLUX.2 and Qwen Image, and the best open editor scores 0.586 on TRACE-Bench against 0.821 for Nano Banana 2 (arXiv 2608.16765), so a platform earns its fee only if golden references still drift after post-processing. That also settles the subscription question. No consumer plan automates this job: the Gemini app and Flow are manual and watermarked, Midjourney has no official API and its terms forbid automation (Unifically), and pay-as-you-go API spend undercuts every platform subscription unless LoRA training becomes necessary.

The tools the user already owns cannot carry the batch either. Figma's Starter plan grants 500 AI credits a month (150 a day), and "Make image" with ChatGPT Images 2.0 costs 2 credits, so about 250 free images a month (Figma Help) that the customer owns (Figma AI Terms); but reference images work only by hand in the Figma UI, and the Figma MCP image tool available in this session accepts nothing but a text prompt. Figma Weave keeps separate credits, 150 a month on the free plan, and reserves its REST API for Enterprise (Figma Weave pricing). Local generation on the user's M1 with 16 GB is the weakest option of all. A base M1 needed about 335 seconds for one FLUX schnell image in 2024 (mflux), extrapolations put today's lighter models at 1.5–4 minutes per image or 3–8 hours per pass, and the best commercially licensed open editor, the Apache-licensed Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 (Hugging Face), needs a 20 GB quantized file against about 22 GiB of free disk and accumulates errors sharply across edit turns (arXiv 2509.13399).

Licences add traps for anyone tempted by open weights: FLUX.2 klein 9B and Qwen-Image 2.1 are non-commercial (VentureBeat; Qwen licence), as are BRIA's RMBG-2.0 background-removal weights (GitHub), and if an open pipeline is ever needed, a rented GPU trains a FLUX.2 klein 4B LoRA in about an hour for roughly $0.50 (Hugging Face). The other names drop out on specific grounds. Adobe Firefly reportedly requires a $1,000 monthly minimum, and its indemnity may not cover outputs built from uploaded references (Redress Compliance); Reve's official API was reportedly shut down on 14 August 2026 (eesel); Recraft V4 Styles reproduces a style "rather than editing" an image (OpenRouter); Ideogram prices character reference only on its older 3.0 model (Puter); and Stability offers no multi-reference identity endpoint (Puter).

A $6 test run picks the fusion model before any full pass

The test run should spend its money where the models differ most. Ten jobs cover the risks: one full growth chain of stage 1 plus three edits (the ice orca's accumulating frost crystals make drift easy to spot), three fusions with clashing body plans such as orca with bird, one soft-vinyl portrait, one rimless chibi enemy and one halo. Every model should receive the same parent images, for instance crops from the existing concept sheets in reports/kv, so that differences come from the model rather than its inputs. The portrait earns its slot because turning a flat sticker into a 3D vinyl render is a cross-domain conversion, and the MultiBanana benchmark found that every model scores lower on cross-domain tasks (arXiv 2511.22989).

Run the set on four models. GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst runs at "high", with extra "medium" and "max" runs on the three fusions to price the quality curve, since finished sprites are downscaled to 256–512 px and "medium" costs a quarter of "high". MAI-Image-2.6 runs through the new OpenRouter adapter, and Muse Image rides the same adapter for about ten cents. Nano Banana 2 runs through the existing Vertex key, which doubles as the test of whether the Cloud billing console shows the $10 credit absorbing the charge. At list prices, one candidate per job across all four costs about $3, and two candidates about $6, inside the $10 monthly cap; Seedream 5.0 Pro is optional because it needs its own BytePlus call.

Judge the results on the contact sheet the pipeline already renders, asking whether stage 4 still reads as the stage-1 creature, whether each fusion takes its body plan from parent A and the extra part from parent B, whether the cut-out edge survives on black, where halos invisible on white show up (Runware), and how much palette-snap correction each output needed. Human eyes should make the call, because standard CLIP and DINO identity scores agree with human raters at a Krippendorff α of only about 0.3 unless they are masked to the subject (MaSC); automated scores can rank candidates but not approve them. One practitioner's rule, checking the first five images against a checklist before running a batch, is the right gate between test run and full pass (Little Might), and the winners should become the goldens the pipeline already looks for in reports/kv/approved/, a folder that does not exist yet.

Two pipeline changes cost nothing and reduce drift whichever model wins. The baby-stage jobs currently send only the previous stage as a reference, the worst case for compounding drift; also sending the approved stage-1 image, and repeating the preserve list word for word, follows OpenAI's own anti-drift advice (OpenAI cookbook) and fits every shortlisted model's reference slots. Line weight, which sticker-set makers see drifting early alongside the eyes (stampo.jp), can be made identical on every keyed sprite by re-stroking the outline from the alpha mask during post-processing, exactly as palette snapping already does for colour. After the test run, give each asset class to one model, because GPT-drawn and MAI-drawn fusions side by side would show a visible seam.

The rollout has to follow the dependency graph. Fusions need both parents' stage-4 sprites and portraits need their fusions, so a pass runs in about seven waves, and fully batched waves of up to 24 hours each could stretch one pass across days while saving only cents on the narrow eight-image chain waves. The sensible split is to run the chain synchronously and batch the wide waves of fusions with halos, portraits and enemies. At standard "high" rates a GPT pass breaks the $10 cap, so either batch the wide waves or raise the cap for non-Google vendors, and feed OpenAI's token usage or OpenRouter's usage.cost into the ledger, which today prices any unknown model at $0.134. If zero spend beyond AI Pro is a hard rule, run the Vertex lane first and keep GPT Image 2.5 as the fallback; the ranking stands, and only the order of attempts changes.

Conclusion

The research moves the question from which model is cheapest to which failure is most expensive. Every credible engine costs under about $15 a pass, and the spread between the leader and the cheapest serious option is a few dollars, trivial next to the hours already lost to quota errors or an afternoon spent hand-curating drift. The AI Pro subscription, meanwhile, contributes to this pipeline only through its $10 credit, since the Gemini app and Flow are manual and watermarked and the AI Studio playground refused image generation outright. If the test run's Vertex call does not visibly draw that credit down, the subscription buys nothing for asset production, and its cost is better redirected to direct API spend.

The deeper implication is that consistency should live in code wherever possible. Anchored chain references, explicit fusion roles, palette snapping and outline re-stroking turn the model into a replaceable part, and that matters in a market this unstable: gpt-image-2 gained transparency as a preview on 21 August and lost it on 1 October (The Decoder; OpenAI community), gpt-image-1 retires on 23 October (CostGoat), and Reve's API reportedly disappeared in August. The pipeline's backend map is already halfway to a references-in, PNG-out interface; finishing that abstraction, rather than marrying any one vendor, is the durable win.
